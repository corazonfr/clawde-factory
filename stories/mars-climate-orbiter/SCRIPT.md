## Voice-over (as sent to ElevenLabs)
NASA once flew a spacecraft all the way to Mars, and lost it to a unit conversion.
December 1998, Florida. The Mars Climate Orbiter takes off to study the weather on Mars. A company built it, and wrote part of its software. NASA's navigators steer it from the ground.
On the way, sunlight presses on its lopsided solar panel and slowly twists it. So, every so often, small thrusters puff to straighten it out. And each puff nudges it slightly off course. The company's software reports every puff. In pound-force seconds. The navigators' software reads them. As newton-seconds.
A pound of force is about four and a half newtons. So every push was four and a half times bigger than anyone thought.
The plan was to arrive two hundred and twenty-six kilometres above Mars. Below eighty, it wouldn't survive. It came in at about fifty-seven.
On the twenty-third of September, it slipped behind the planet, forty-nine seconds early. It never came back out.
Luckily, nobody was on board. Unluckily, it was also meant to be the radio for a lander arriving that December.
Imagine pushing a shopping cart while someone keeps nudging it. Each nudge, you're told, is tiny. Really, it's four and a half times bigger. One nudge, nobody notices. Nine months of nudges, and you're in the wrong aisle.
The investigation said the real failure wasn't the arithmetic. It was that nobody caught it.
It was sent to study the Martian air. Its last reading was extremely close up.
