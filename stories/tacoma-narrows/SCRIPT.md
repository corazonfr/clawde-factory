## Voice-over (as sent to ElevenLabs)
In 1940, a wind of forty-two miles an hour tore a brand-new steel bridge to pieces.
The Tacoma Narrows Bridge, Washington State. Opened in July. The third-longest suspension bridge in the world. And it bounced.
Even in a light wind, the road rolled up and down in waves. Locals called it Galloping Gertie, and drove out just to ride it.
On the seventh of November, the wind picked up. The road was rolling again. Then it began to twist. One side up, the other side down, like someone wringing out a towel. A driver left his car in the middle and walked off.
At two minutes past eleven, six hundred feet of road dropped a hundred and ninety-five feet into the water.
Luckily, it was all caught on film. Unluckily, the people filming were standing on the bridge.
The problem was the shape. The sides of the road were solid steel walls, eight feet tall, so the wind couldn't get through. Hold a strip of paper under your lip and blow. It doesn't just bend. It flaps. Every twist tilted the road into the wind, and the wind twisted it further.
The new bridge, built in 1950, has an open steel frame, so the wind blows straight through.
The old one is still down there, two hundred feet under the water, home to octopus and fish. It finally learned to lie still.
