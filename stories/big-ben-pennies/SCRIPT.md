## Voice-over (as sent to ElevenLabs)
Britain's most famous clock is kept on time by a little stack of old pennies.
London, 1859. The Great Clock at Westminster. Four faces, minute hands fourteen feet long, and a promise: right to within one second a day.
Then the weather got involved. In August 1949, a whole flock of starlings landed on one minute hand.
Britain's most famous clock fell four and a half minutes behind.
Luckily, starlings are tiny. Unluckily, there were a lot of starlings.
On New Year's Eve 1962, snow and ice piled onto the hands, and Britain's new year arrived late.
So how do you fine-tune a clock that big? With pocket change.
Think of a playground swing. Shorter ropes swing faster. On top of the pendulum sits a stack of pennies. Add one, and the weight sits a tiny bit higher, so the pendulum swings a tiny bit faster. One penny makes the clock gain about two-fifths of a second a day.
A penny on to speed it up. A penny off to slow it down.
In 2009, three of the pennies were swapped for one five-pound coin, made for the London Olympics.
The most precise clock in Britain, and it runs on small change.
