## Voice-over (as sent to ElevenLabs)
In 1861, a brick hotel in Chicago rose six feet into the air. Some of the guests didn't notice.
Chicago, the eighteen-fifties. A booming city built on a swamp, barely higher than Lake Michigan. Rain had nowhere to go. Water stood in the streets, and disease came with it. In 1854, cholera killed six percent of the city.
So in 1856, an engineer made a plan. Lay sewer pipes along the streets. Bury them under several feet of soil. Which raised the streets, and left the buildings sitting in holes.
So the buildings went up too. A four-story brick building, seven hundred and fifty tons, on two hundred jackscrews. Then half a city block, four feet eight inches in five days, with six thousand jackscrews. The shops stayed open.
Then the hotel. Five hundred men. A thousand jackscrews. Six feet up.
Luckily, wooden buildings were easier. Unluckily, they were put on rollers and moved to the edge of town, sometimes with the shop still open.
A jackscrew is the jack you change a car tire with. Each turn lifts a tiny bit. Put a thousand under a hotel, turn them slowly, and the walls don't crack.
The new sewers worked. They drained into the river. Which drained into the lake. Which was the drinking water.
So in 1900, Chicago dug a canal and made the river flow backwards.
First they lifted the city. Then they turned the river around.
